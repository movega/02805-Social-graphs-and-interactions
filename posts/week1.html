Week 01 Degree distributions

Mapping the Marvel Network

Bootstrap edition: we load the frozen snapshot of Wikipedia’s Marvel superheroes, count who links to whom, and see what the shape of the degree distribution already gives away.

07/09/2026 Alvaro Vega, Oier Garcia & Paula Balcells Dataset: week-1 release · 303 nodes · 1,784 directed edges
The Marvel popularity trap

At first glance, the most famous Marvel superheroes should also be the most central ones. But the Wikipedia network tells a more complicated story: some are frequently linked to, some link out to many others, and some do both. We wanted to find out whether popularity and connectedness really point to the same characters.

What we asked

The main topic of this network analysis was "The Marvel popularity trap", in which we tried to answer whether the most famous names really sit at the center of the network by more than one definition of centrality.

What we did

To answer our question and investigate the Marvel popularity trap, we first started with the undirected version of the network, and tried to answer how many distinvt neighbors does each character have, in order to see who was the most connected character. Spider-Man dominated this view, followed by Hulk and Wolverine.

We then looked at the directed version of the network, and counted how many incoming links (in-degree) each character had, in order to see who was the most referenced character. In this view, Spider-Man was still the most referenced character, followed by the same two characters, Hulk and Wolverine. Finally, we looked at the directed version of the network again, but this time we counted how many outgoing links (out-degree) each character had, in order to see who was the most referencing character. In this view, the ranking was led by Betsy Braddock, Cloack and Dagger, and Adam Warlock.

In order to go a bit further, we then created some "link personalities" to characterize each character by lookin at how much it receives versus emits. We visualized this by plotting the out-degree vs the in-degree. We then used percentile-based thresholds (rather than arbirtrary fixed degree thresholds) and defined the following network archetypes:

  • Celebrities are characters that receive a lot of attention but do not reference many others.
  • Connectors are characters that receive a lot of attention and also reference many others.
  • Socialites are characters that do not receive much attention but reference many others.
  • Outsiders are characters that do not receive much attention and do not reference many others.

From this analysis, we got some examples from each archetype. For example, Spider-Man was a celebrity, while Hulk was a socialite. Betsy Braddock was instead a connector, and the outsiders included Doctor Nemesis.

Finally, we looked at the forgotten heroes, those are characters that are mostly peripheral to this 303-character Marvel universe. We focused on the edge cases: isolates, degree-1 nodes, and members of tiny components (less than 5 nodes) outside the giant network.

What surprised us

The biggest surprise was that the answer changed as soon as we paid attention to direction. Spider-Man, Hulk, and Wolverine were at the top when we counted incoming links and distinct neighbors, but the out-degree ranking was led by Betsy Braddock, Cloak and Dagger, and Adam Warlock. In other words, the characters most often referenced by the network were not necessarily the characters whose pages referenced the widest part of the network.

That difference is a reminder that this graph describes Wikipedia’s structure, not fictional relationships. Incoming links may reflect broad recognition, while outgoing links can reflect how an article is written and how many related characters its editors choose to mention. The same superhero can therefore look central under one definition and much less central under another.

In the Marvel Wikipedia universe, every superhero has a link personality: some attract attention, some spread it, some do both, and some stay on the edge.
